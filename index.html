How It Works Under the Hood

Plain-English explanations of what happens inside those black and white squares.

Inside the Code: Your Message vs. Backup Repair Data

Every 8 squares inside the grid form one byte (a single character or number). Your text is stored in blue message bytes, followed by amber backup bytes generated using error-correcting math (called Reed–Solomon coding—the same technology that keeps scratched Blu-ray discs and deep-space NASA probes readable).

When you spill coffee on the grid, any byte whose squares flip color gets a red border. As long as no more than half of the backup bytes in a group are damaged, the scanner solves a set of equations to pinpoint which bytes changed and restores them (green ring).

Why Scratching a Corner Breaks It Instantly

1. Corner targets have zero backup data: The three big squares in the corners are alignment bullseyes. They tell your phone camera where the QR code is, how big it is, and whether you're holding your phone upside down. Because they hold no message bytes, error correction cannot rebuild them if they're scratched out.

2. Why scattered speckles hurt more than one solid blob: A QR code repairs damage in whole bytes (clusters of 8 squares), not individual pixels. One solid coffee blob destroys a handful of bytes completely, leaving the rest untouched. The exact same amount of coffee scattered as tiny speckles nicks dozens of separate bytes at once—using up the repair limit much faster.

Why QR Codes Always Look Like Even Static

Raw message data often creates big empty white patches or stripes that confuse phone cameras. Before finishing a QR code, the generator tests 8 different checkerboard/stripe stencils (called masks) to scramble the squares evenly, and automatically picks whichever pattern looks cleanest and most balanced.
