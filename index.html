Spill coffee on a QR code. Why does it still scan?

A QR code isn't just your message drawn as squares. A big part of it is spare information computed from your message, so a phone can work out which squares are wrong, and what they should have been, without being told where the damage is.

Go deeper

The bytes the scanner read, and the ones it fixed

Your message is split into data bytes, and each block gets repair bytes. Red outline = a byte your coffee changed. Green ring = a byte the decoder located and corrected. Hover any byte for its value.

The syndromes are the decoder's first check: it evaluates the block as a polynomial at special points. All zero means "this block is a valid codeword, nothing wrong". Anything non-zero is a fingerprint of the errors, and from it the decoder solves for where they are and what they should be.

Why do QR codes look so random? (the 8 masks)

Raw data can accidentally contain big empty patches, long stripes, or a pattern that looks like a corner finder (dark-light-dark-dark-dark-light-dark). Those confuse cameras. So the encoder XORs the data area with each of 8 fixed patterns, scores every result with penalty rules, and keeps the lowest. The chosen mask number is written into the format info so the scanner can undo it. Click a mask to force it. The damage you painted stays.

The math: how bytes become polynomials

Each byte is treated as a number in GF(256), a 256-element number system where adding is XOR and multiplying never overflows past a byte. A block of data bytes becomes the coefficients of a polynomial. The repair bytes are the remainder when that polynomial (shifted up) is divided by a fixed generator polynomial. That makes the whole block divisible by the generator, so it equals zero at the generator's roots α⁰, α¹, α², …

Damage breaks that divisibility. Plugging the roots into the damaged block gives the syndromes. With n repair bytes you get n equations. Each unknown error has two unknowns (its position and its value), so n repair bytes can fix n/2 errors at unknown positions. That's why level H's "30%" means 30% of bytes, and why the budget squares above are half the number of repair bytes.

The decoder in this page runs the classic steps: Berlekamp–Massey (finds the error-locator polynomial), Chien search (its roots are the error positions), Forney's formula (the error values). All of it is in qr_engine.py.

What the corner squares (and other fixed patterns) do

Finder patterns (the three big corner squares) are how a camera finds the code in a photo, and how it learns its size, angle and which way is up. Their 1:1:3:1:1 stripe ratio is the same in any direction. They contain no message data, so no repair byte protects them. Scanners vary: many need all three, some can guess one back. This simulation counts a finder as lost once 8 of its 49 squares are wrong.

Format info (15 bits along the finders) says which error-correction level and mask were used. It's written twice, and has its own small error-correcting code, so the decoder picks the nearest valid pattern. Timing stripes help count rows and columns. Alignment squares help with bent or tilted codes.

This is also why scattered speckles hurt more than one blob: errors are counted in bytes, not squares. Each byte is 8 squares packed close together, so one blob ruins a few bytes completely, while the same number of speckles nicks a different byte each time.

Everything on this page is computed live from your input by a from-scratch QR encoder and Reed–Solomon decoder written in Python (qr_engine.py), running in your browser through Pyodide. No libraries do the QR math. The "second opinion" uses jsQR, an independent open-source scanner, as a cross-check. Tip: turn off "Show repaired bytes" and point your phone camera at the screen.
