Under the Hood

Inspect the live Galois Field bytes, syndromes, XOR masks, and polynomial math computed by our Python engine.

Live Codeword Bytes & Error Syndromes

Your message is encoded into data bytes, followed by parity repair bytes. A red border marks a byte altered by your spill; a green ring marks a byte algebraically repaired by Berlekamp–Massey + Forney. Hover any hex byte to compare raw vs. corrected values.

Syndromes are the decoder's diagnostic fingerprint: it evaluates the received block polynomial at generator roots α⁰, α¹, …. All 00 means zero detectable errors. Non-zero syndromes form a system of equations solved to find both error locations and error magnitudes.

Why QR Codes Look Random (The 8 XOR Masks)

Unmasked data can accidentally form large blank blobs, long stripes, or fake 1:1:3:1:1 finder patterns that confuse cameras. The encoder XORs the data region against 8 deterministic bit-patterns, scores every candidate using ISO penalty rules, and picks the lowest score. Click any mask below to force it while keeping your spill intact.

The Math: Polynomial Division in GF(2⁸)

Every byte is treated as an element of the finite field GF(256) (modulo primitive polynomial 0x11D), where addition is bitwise XOR and multiplication never overflows 8 bits. A block of message bytes forms a polynomial M(x), and the repair bytes are the remainder of M(x)·xⁿ ÷ g(x).

Because each unknown error has two unknowns (its byte position and its XOR error magnitude), n parity bytes yield n syndrome equations and can repair up to ⌊n/2⌋ corrupted bytes at unknown positions. Our decoder executes Berlekamp–Massey (error-locator polynomial), Chien search (roots = positions), and Forney's algorithm (error values) directly in qr_engine.py.

Why Corner Squares Are Unprotected (Anatomy)

Finder patterns (the three 7×7 corner targets) carry zero message bits—they exist so a camera can locate the code in a frame and determine orientation, scale, and perspective skew via their invariant 1:1:3:1:1 stripe ratio. Because they hold no codewords, Reed–Solomon cannot repair them.

Format info (15 bits hugging the finders) stores the error-correction level and mask ID, protected by its own BCH(15,5) code and written twice for redundancy.

Why scattered speckles overwhelm a single blob: Reed–Solomon counts errors in whole bytes (8 adjacent modules typically packed in a 2×4 zig-zag column). One solid blob destroys a few bytes completely, whereas the same area scattered as speckles nicks dozens of separate bytes—exhausting the block's repair budget much faster.
