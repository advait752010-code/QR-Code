How the Repair Works: Live Step-by-Step Trace & Codeword Bytes

Every 8 squares inside the data area form one byte. Your message is stored in blue message bytes, followed by amber backup bytes calculated from your message so a scanner can detect which bytes changed and reconstruct their original values.

Technical Details (Finite Field & Polynomial Math)

Before printing, each group's message bytes are treated as coefficients of a polynomial over the finite field GF(256) (modulo primitive polynomial 0x11D). Dividing by a generator polynomial produces the amber backup bytes (the remainder). Because each unknown error has two unknowns (its byte position and its XOR bit-flip value), n backup bytes yield n syndrome equations and can repair up to ‚åän/2‚åã damaged bytes using Berlekamp‚ÄìMassey, Chien search, and Forney's algorithm.

Why Location Matters: Protected Data vs. Structural Targets

Corner targets carry zero backup data: The three 7√ó7 bullseye squares in the corners tell your phone camera where the QR code is, its scale, and its orientation via their 1:1:3:1:1 stripe ratio. Because they hold no message bytes, error correction cannot rebuild them if scratched out.

Why scattered speckles hurt more than one solid blob: Error correction counts damage in whole 8-bit bytes (usually a 2√ó4 cluster of squares), not individual pixels. One solid blob destroys a few bytes completely, whereas the same area scattered as speckles nicks dozens of separate bytes at once, exhausting the repair budget much faster.

Why QR Codes Look Like Even Static (The 8 XOR Masks)

Unmasked data can accidentally form large blank blobs or fake corner targets that confuse cameras. The encoder XORs the data region against 8 deterministic bit-patterns, scores every candidate using ISO penalty rules (runs, 2√ó2 blocks, finder look-alikes, dark/light balance), and picks the lowest-clutter pattern.
